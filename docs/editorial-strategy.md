# Editorial strategy — Chef Peter the Teacher

How the Culinary Library is written, checked and published. Use this with:

- `docs/editorial-calendar.md` — the first 28 article briefs and suggested dates
- **Admin → Editorial calendar** — the same briefs in the CMS, with a button that creates each draft
- `docs/seo-strategy.md` — keyword themes and how articles support the service pages
- `docs/CONTENT_REVIEW.md` — facts about Chef Peter that are still awaiting his confirmation

---

## 1. Purpose

The library should become a place where cooks, bakers, students and food businesses come back to **learn**, because the lessons are accurate, practical and clearly explained. Trust in the library supports Chef Peter’s reputation as a chef and educator; it should never be used to make claims about him that he has not confirmed.

**Success looks like:** readers finish articles, try the practical lessons, move from one article to the next and, where it fits their needs, enquire about training or consultancy. It does not depend on publishing volume.

## 2. Readers

| Reader | What they need from us |
|---|---|
| Home cooks and home bakers in Uganda | Clear methods, locally available ingredients, reasons why things go wrong |
| Culinary and hospitality students | Correct terminology, principles behind techniques, exam-friendly structure |
| Junior cooks and kitchen staff | Professional standards: safety, organisation, speed, consistency |
| Food business owners and managers (restaurants, hotels, bakeries, caterers) | Systems, costing, hygiene and training that work in practice |
| International readers | An honest, respectful introduction to Ugandan and East African food |

Each brief names its main reader. Write for that person.

## 3. Editorial identity and voice

The library speaks as **an experienced chef and culinary teacher explaining things to a capable learner**: warm, direct and precise.

**The writing is:**

- **Educational.** Every article has one clear learning objective, stated in its brief.
- **Natural and conversational.** Use short sentences, plain words and "you". Read it aloud; if it sounds like a brochure, rewrite it.
- **Professionally accurate.** Use correct culinary terms, explain each one the first time it appears, and check every figure.
- **Practical.** Show a real kitchen situation, a step, a measurement or a test the reader can do.
- **Interesting.** Include the reason behind the method, a short piece of history, or the mistake that teaches the lesson.
- **Respectful of every food culture.** Name the country or community a dish belongs to. Avoid "exotic", "ethnic" or "authentic" used as a judgement. Present variations as variations, not errors.

**Avoid:**

- Generic openings. Don't write "In today's fast-paced world…", "Cooking is an art…" or "Have you ever wondered…?". Start with the problem, the scene or the fact.
- Repetitive conclusions. Don't repeat the introduction. End with what the reader can now do, and a useful next link.
- Filler and hype: "ultimate", "secret", "game-changing", "mouth-watering", stacked adjectives.
- Over-claiming: "always", "never" and "the only way", unless they are genuinely true (as they sometimes are in food safety).

**Example — opening paragraph**

> ✗ *Bread is one of the oldest foods known to humankind, and it has been enjoyed for thousands of years across the globe.*
>
> ✓ *Flour, water and salt make a flat, dense dough. Add a small amount of yeast, wait, and the same dough becomes light and springy. Understanding why helps you control your bread instead of hoping it rises.*

## 4. Chef Peter’s voice — labelled boxes

Articles may include four labelled boxes (they display as coloured panels; type the label at the start of a quote, or use **Insert box…** in the editor):

| Label | Use for | Who may write it |
|---|---|---|
| **Chef Peter’s tip:** | Personal advice from Chef Peter | **Only Chef Peter, or text he has read and approved** |
| **What professional chefs should know:** | Professional standards and context | Editorial team |
| **Common kitchen mistakes:** | What goes wrong and how to avoid it | Editorial team |
| **Practical kitchen lesson:** | A short exercise the reader can do | Editorial team |

The existing **Chef’s tip**, **Chef’s note**, **Safety note** and **In the Ugandan kitchen** boxes remain available.

**Attribution rule.** Personal experiences, opinions, stories and quotations are attributed to Chef Peter **only after he has confirmed them in writing** (an email or WhatsApp message kept on file is enough). The drafts leave every "Chef Peter’s tip" box as a bracketed note for him to complete or delete. Never write "Chef Peter recalls…", "In Chef Peter’s experience…" or similar without that confirmation.

Do not create author biographies, credentials or achievements in articles. The author box uses the approved profile from **Admin → Author profile**.

## 5. Writing standards

Every article must:

1. **Have a clear educational objective.** It is in the brief, and the reader should be able to state it after reading.
2. **Answer the reader’s likely questions.** Use the main and supporting keywords as a guide to what people ask, not as words to repeat.
3. **Explain technical terms** the first time they appear, in plain English.
4. **Include practical examples**, preferably from kitchens readers recognise: charcoal stoves, local markets, warm climates, power cuts, hotel and restaurant kitchens.
5. **Have a useful structure.** Use short sections with H2 headings (three or more create a table of contents), numbered steps for methods, and bullet lists for checklists. Avoid tables, because the editor does not keep them; use a structured list instead.
6. **Avoid repetition.** Make each point once, in the best place.
7. **Link to relevant resources:** related library articles, the matching class or consultancy page, and the relevant book. Links are listed in each brief. Link to a planned article only after it is published.
8. **Cite reliable references for factual claims** in a "References" section at the end.

**Length.** Long enough to teach the topic properly. Most lessons will be roughly 900–1,800 words. Don't pad an article to reach a number.

## 6. Accuracy and references

**Acceptable sources:** official guidance (WHO, FAO/Codex Alimentarius, national food authorities), standard professional textbooks, peer-reviewed research, and museum or university publications for history.

**Never** invent a reference, quote, statistic, date or study. If a source cannot be found, remove the claim. The calendar marks topics where the editor still has to find a source ("Editor to source").

### Food safety

- Base every rule, temperature and time on authoritative guidance. The core sources are:
  - WHO *Five Keys to Safer Food Manual*;
  - Codex *General Principles of Food Hygiene* (CXC 1-1969, Rev. 2022), which includes HACCP;
  - the guidance of national food authorities.
- Quote figures exactly as the source gives them, and name the source.
- Do not give medical advice. For suspected food poisoning, advise readers to seek medical care.
- Legal and certification requirements differ by country. Mention Ugandan requirements only with a reference to the relevant Ugandan authority.

### Food history and culture

- Separate three kinds of statement clearly:
  1. **Established fact:** "Historians agree…", with a source.
  2. **Debated interpretation:** "Some researchers argue…; others…".
  3. **Tradition or popular story:** "According to a popular story…".
- Customs differ between communities and families. Describe them as examples, not universal rules.
- Credit community knowledge to its source, and share it only with permission.

### Recipes

- Use tested quantities and methods from a reliable source, or recipes Chef Peter supplies.
- Do not say that Chef Peter developed or tested a recipe unless he confirms it.
- Use the editor’s recipe fields (ingredients, method, times, yield) only with real values. Recipe structured data is generated only when the recipe is complete (see `docs/seo-system.md`).
- Give metric weights first, and note substitutions only where they have been checked.

## 7. Photographs

- Each brief suggests photographs. Use Chef Peter’s own photographs or images with a clear licence; record the credit in the Media library.
- Every image needs **alt text** that describes what it shows, and a caption where it helps.
- Photographs of identifiable people (students, staff, clients) need their consent.
- Do not use stock images in a way that suggests they show Chef Peter, his kitchen or his students.

## 8. Publication workflow

```
Brief (calendar) → Draft created in CMS → Writing & fact-check → Chef Peter review → Final edit → Scheduled → Published
```

| Step | Who | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Create the draft | Administrator | **Admin → Editorial calendar → Create draft.** The first four articles open as full drafts; the others as outlines with writing notes. The article is created as a **draft only**. |
| 2. Write and fact-check | Writer / editor | Complete every `[EDITOR: …]`, `[REFERENCE: …]` and `[PHOTO: …]` note. Check every figure against its source. Add the featured image and alt text. |
| 3. Chef Peter review | Chef Peter | Reads the draft (use **Preview**). Writes or approves the "Chef Peter’s tip" box, or asks for it to be removed. Corrects anything that does not match his practice. |
| 4. Final edit and approval | Editor / administrator | Checks the SEO panel, links and layout on mobile. Approval = scheduling. |
| 5. Schedule | Administrator | Set the date and time in the editor and choose **Schedule**. Articles publish automatically at that time. |

**Safeguards built into the CMS**

- Nothing in the calendar publishes or schedules an article automatically.
- An article containing any `[EDITOR: …]`, `[CHEF PETER: …]`, `[PHOTO: …]` or `[REFERENCE: …]` note **cannot be published or scheduled**. The editor explains what is left.
- The SEO panel shows the unfinished notes as an error.
- Creating a draft whose address already exists is refused, so duplicates are not created.

**Recommended rhythm:** two quality articles a week, on Tuesdays and Fridays, published in the morning (Kampala time). Keep at least one week of approved articles scheduled ahead. If an article is not ready, move it; never publish an unchecked article to keep the schedule.

## 9. Pre-publication checklist

- [ ] The learning objective is clear in the first few paragraphs.
- [ ] Every technical term is explained.
- [ ] Every factual claim has a reliable source, listed under References.
- [ ] Food-safety figures match the cited guidance exactly.
- [ ] History separates fact, debate and tradition.
- [ ] Nothing is attributed to Chef Peter without his confirmation.
- [ ] No bracketed notes remain.
- [ ] The featured image has alt text; photographs have credit and consent where needed.
- [ ] Internal links work and point to published pages.
- [ ] The excerpt / meta description is 120–160 characters and specific.
- [ ] The article reads well on a phone.

## 10. After publication

- **Share** each new article on Chef Peter’s confirmed social channels with the article’s own link.
- **Review** each article every 12 months. Re-check food-safety articles whenever the cited guidance is revised.
- **Measure** with free tools once the owner has approved Search Console: which articles people find, which searches bring them, and which articles lead to enquiries. Use this to choose topics for the next calendar, not to chase keywords.
- **Plan the next 28** about two months before the current calendar ends. Use readers’ questions, enquiries and the gaps in `docs/seo-strategy.md`.
